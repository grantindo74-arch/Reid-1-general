Chapter 01 · Networks

How Cell Networks Work

You send a text and it shows up on your friend's phone almost instantly. Here's every stop it makes on the way.

1.1 The journey 1.2 Infographic 1.3 Phone missions
Lesson 1.1

The journey of a text

Stop 1 · Your phone

A radio in your pocket

Your phone turns your message into radio waves and sends it out. Your SIM card is your ID: it holds a secret key that proves to the network you're a real customer and is used to encrypt everything you send.

Stop 2 · Radio waves

Far and slow, or short and fast

Carriers send data on different frequency bands. Low frequencies travel far and go through walls but carry less data. High frequencies carry tons of data but only go a short distance, and walls, trees, even your hand can block them.

Low-band600–900 MHz
Mid-band1.7–3.8 GHz
mmWave 5G24–39 GHz
Bar length = how far it reachesShorter = faster
Stop 3 · Cell tower

The map is made of cells

Each tower covers an area called a cell, which is where "cellular" comes from. Neighboring cells avoid interfering with each other, so the same frequencies can be reused across a whole city. When you move, your phone keeps measuring nearby towers and the network hands you off to a stronger one without dropping your call.

Stop 4 · Backhaul

Wireless for only the first stretch

The tower sends your message into fiber-optic cable: pulses of light in glass strands. So the "wireless" part of your text is only the trip to the tower. After that it's wires, all the way to the carrier's data center.

Stop 5 · Core network

The carrier's brain

Racks of servers called the core network handle the hard parts. In 5G, the core is mostly software, often running on the same kind of cloud servers as websites and games.

Who are you?Checks your SIM's secret key
Where's your friend?Tracks which tower each phone is near
Send itRoutes your message to the right place
RulesData limits, billing, roaming
Stop 6 · Friend's phone

Delivered

The core finds the tower nearest your friend, and the whole trip runs backwards: fiber to their tower, radio to their phone. Usually it takes under a second, even across the country.

Upgrades over time

1G to 5G

1G
1980s
Analog voice calls only. Anyone with a radio scanner could listen in.
2G
1990s
Calls go digital and text messages are born.
3G
2000s
Real internet on phones. Slow, but it made the first smartphones possible.
4G
2010s
Everything becomes internet data, even voice calls. Video streaming takes off.
5G
2020s
Faster and lower delay, connects far more devices, and the core runs as software in the cloud.
Did you know?
Concerts jam the networkEvery phone in a cell shares the same tower. At a packed stadium, carriers roll in extra towers on trucks called COWs: Cells On Wheels.
Bars are a rough guessPhone makers decide what counts as "4 bars." The real number is signal strength in dBm. You'll measure it yourself in Mission 1.
911 works on any networkIn the US, an emergency call goes through on any carrier's tower that can hear your phone, even if your own carrier has no signal there.
Math corner

Why high frequencies are called "millimeter wave"

Radio waves travel at the speed of light. Divide that speed by the frequency and you get the length of one wave:

wavelength = 300,000,000 m/s ÷ frequency (Hz)

Try 600 (low-band), 2500 (mid-band) and 28000 (mmWave). Longer waves bend around buildings and pass through walls more easily. Tiny ones get blocked.

Lesson 1.3

Phone missions

Five experiments for your Android phone. Check each one off when you finish. Your answers save on this device.

Mission 1

Secret signal meter

5 min · at home

Find your phone's real signal strength, measured in dBm instead of bars.

  1. Open Settings → About phone.
  2. Tap SIM status (on Samsung: Status information → SIM card status). Menus differ a little by brand.
  3. Find Signal strength. It looks like -95 dBm 45 asu. The dBm number is the one you want.

Can't find it? Install the free app Network Cell Info Lite from the Play Store. It shows dBm live.

-120 very weak-100-80 excellent

Enter a reading to see how it rates.

Why it's negative: dBm measures power compared to 1 milliwatt, and your phone receives way less than that. Every 10 dBm closer to zero means 10× more power. So -70 dBm is 1,000× stronger than -100.

Mission 2

Tower hunt

30 min · go outside

Find the cell tower your phone is probably using, then go see it in real life.

  1. Go to cellmapper.net/map. Pick your carrier from the menu.
  2. Find the towers closest to your house. Which one is nearest?
  3. Go find it. Many towers are disguised as trees, flagpoles or church steeples, or hidden on rooftops.

Stay on public sidewalks and don't go past fences. Looking is fine, climbing is not.

What you learn: towers are everywhere once you know how to spot them, and carriers place them carefully to cover every neighborhood.

Mission 3

Signal map of the house

15 min · at home

Use the signal reading from Mission 1 in five different spots. Stand still for 10 seconds before writing each number down.

SpotdBmRank

Your prediction: which spot will be worst? Guess before you measure. Walls, floors, metal appliances and distance from the tower all weaken radio waves.

Mission 4

Speed test showdown

10 min · at home

Compare cell data and Wi-Fi using speedtest.net or the Speedtest app.

  1. Connected to home Wi-Fi: run a test.
  2. Turn Wi-Fi off so you're on cell data: run it again.

A speed test on cell data can use a few hundred MB. Check with Dad about your data plan first.

Download (Mbps)Ping (ms)
Wi-Fi
Cell

Two different things: download speed is how much data per second. Ping is how long a tiny message takes to go there and back. For online games, low ping matters more than raw speed.

Mission 5

Faraday challenge

10 min · needs a helper

Can you block radio waves completely?

  1. Wrap your phone in 3 layers of aluminum foil with no gaps.
  2. Have someone call it. Does it ring, or go straight to voicemail?
  3. Next, try a metal cookie tin or a pot with its lid on.

Don't use a microwave oven for this, even switched off. Foil and pots work fine.

Tin or pot result:

What's happening: metal soaks up and reflects radio waves. A fully closed metal box is called a Faraday cage. It's why your signal dies in elevators. Even a tiny gap can let some signal leak through.
